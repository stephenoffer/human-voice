# Discussion

Prior work on urban heat islands has treated tree canopy as an aggregate:
percentage cover at the neighborhood scale, correlated against surface
temperature. Placement seems to carry more of the effect than coverage does.
Two neighborhoods with the same canopy percentage differed by as much as 2.4°C
in afternoon surface temperature, depending on whether the trees lined streets
or filled parks.

Planting programs are usually evaluated on trees planted per year. That counts
every tree the same. Per unit of canopy, a street tree cooled more than a park
tree did in our sample, and the mechanism is not mysterious: street trees shade
pavement, and pavement is what drives the heat island during the hottest hours
of the afternoon.

Three limitations bear on how far any of this generalizes. We measured across a
single summer, and canopy transpiration varies with precipitation from year to
year. All eleven neighborhoods sit in one metropolitan area, so building
geometry and background climate are held constant rather than tested. Our
surface temperatures come from 30-meter satellite imagery, which is coarse for
street-scale variation.

Attack the temporal question first. Several years of observation would separate
placement from precipitation variability and give planners a defensible basis
for spending a fixed budget. Arid cities would be worth adding too. Irrigation
cost may change the arithmetic enough to reverse the recommendation entirely,
and we cannot tell from here.
