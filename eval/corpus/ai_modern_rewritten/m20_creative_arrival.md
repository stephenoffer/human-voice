The train ran forty minutes late. By the time Oskar stepped down onto the
platform the light had gone flat and grey, that hour when the afternoon is over
and the evening has not started. He set the case down and looked for the sign
he had been told to look for.

A woman in a green coat held a piece of cardboard with a name on it. Not his.
She caught him reading it and shrugged, apologetic, as if the mismatch were
somehow her doing.

He had rehearsed this arrival for weeks. In the rehearsal there was a car, and
a short drive, and a conversation in which he explained himself well.

Instead there was a platform.

The station café was open, so he bought a coffee he did not want and sat where
he could watch the entrance while the grey deepened, and somewhere in there he
understood that he would have to decide something before dark. The decision
itself was easy. Making it meant admitting the trip had been a mistake from the
start, and he was not going to admit that at four in the afternoon in a town he
had never seen. So he sat. The coffee went cold in front of him and the
entrance stayed empty and the light kept going.

At half past five he picked up the case and walked out into the street. The
woman in the green coat had gone. Her sign leaned against a bench, face up, the
name on it still waiting.
