# On the replication of priming effects in economic games

The literature here is a mess, and pretending otherwise does nobody any
favors. Between 2008 and 2014 something like sixty papers reported that
priming participants with money-related words shifted behavior in dictator and
ultimatum games. Effect sizes clustered around d = 0.4. Since 2015, large
preregistered replications have found almost nothing: the two biggest, with
combined n above 4,000, report effects indistinguishable from zero.

There are three ways to read this and only one of them is popular.

The first reading is that the original effects were never real, and that
publication incentives plus flexible analysis produced a literature out of
noise. This is the standard account. It fits the funnel plot asymmetry well.

The second reading is that the effect is real but small and context-dependent,
and the replications used student samples in settings where money was already
salient, so the prime had nothing left to do. I find this less persuasive than
its defenders do, but it is not stupid, and the two replications that did
recruit non-student samples had noticeably wider confidence intervals.

The third reading is that "priming" was never one phenomenon. Semantic priming
in lexical decision tasks replicates fine. Behavioral priming does not.
Treating these as the same literature because they share a word is the kind of
category error that survives because it is convenient for citation counts.

My own position, for what it is worth after ten years of watching this: the
third reading is the useful one, and the field's failure to make that
distinction cost us most of a decade. I would not fund a new behavioral priming
study. I would fund someone willing to specify, in advance and in mechanistic
terms, what a prime is supposed to be doing.
