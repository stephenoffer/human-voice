# Expanding into the mid-market segment

Our current customer base is concentrated in enterprise accounts, with 78% of
revenue coming from organizations above 5,000 employees. This concentration
creates two problems. The first is revenue risk: losing a single enterprise
account can represent 4% of annual recurring revenue. The second is growth
ceiling, as the enterprise segment in our category contains roughly 2,400
addressable organizations and we already serve 190 of them.

The mid-market segment, defined here as 200 to 5,000 employees, contains
approximately 31,000 addressable organizations. What makes this segment
attractive is not simply its size but its purchasing behavior, which
consistently shows shorter sales cycles and lower customer acquisition cost.
Our own pilot data supports this: the eleven mid-market accounts we closed
opportunistically last year averaged 34 days from first contact to signature,
compared with 187 days for enterprise.

The obstacle is that our product assumes an enterprise deployment model. Setup
requires a dedicated administrator, single sign-on configuration, and a data
migration step that typically takes two weeks of professional services time.
Mid-market buyers do not have a dedicated administrator, and they will not pay
for professional services, meaning the current onboarding path is effectively a
disqualifier for the entire segment.

Addressing this requires three changes. Self-service onboarding must replace the
guided setup, allowing a new customer to reach first value without a call.
Pricing must move from annual contracts to monthly billing, reducing the
commitment required to start. Support must shift from named account managers to
a pooled model, keeping the cost structure viable at a lower average contract
value.

The investment required is approximately $2.1 million across two quarters,
primarily engineering headcount for the self-service work. Our model projects
break-even at 340 mid-market accounts, which at the pilot conversion rate would
take between five and seven quarters. The risk is that our enterprise motion
degrades while attention is divided, and it is a real risk that we should plan
against rather than dismiss.
