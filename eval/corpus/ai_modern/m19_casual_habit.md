# What I learned from tracking my reading for a year

I started tracking every book I read in January, mostly out of curiosity about
whether my sense of my own habits matched the record. It did not, and the gap
turned out to be more interesting than the total.

What I expected was that I would read fewer books than I thought. The opposite
happened. I finished 34 books, which is more than I would have guessed, and the
reason my estimate was low is that I was only counting the ones I remembered
discussing with someone. Books I read and never mentioned had effectively
disappeared from my own accounting of the year.

The distribution was the surprise. Nineteen of the 34 were finished in three
clusters of two weeks each, and the rest were spread thinly across the
remaining ten months. Those clusters lined up with travel and with a stretch in
February when a project stalled, giving me long uninterrupted evenings I would
not otherwise have had. What this suggests is that my reading is governed by
available blocks of time rather than by intention, which is a less flattering
explanation than the one I had been telling myself.

I also tracked abandonment, which is the part I would recommend to anyone
trying this. I started 51 books and finished 34, meaning a third of what I
picked up did not hold me. Looking at the list of seventeen, the pattern is
that almost all of them were books I chose because I felt I should read them.
The ones I finished were the ones I actually wanted.

Would I keep tracking? Probably not in the same detail. The useful information
came out in the first six months, and the last stretch was maintenance on a
spreadsheet nobody was going to read. But I am keeping the abandonment column.
