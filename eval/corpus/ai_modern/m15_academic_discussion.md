# Discussion

Our findings extend prior work on urban heat island mitigation by demonstrating
that tree canopy placement matters more than total canopy coverage. Previous
studies have generally treated canopy as an aggregate variable, measuring
percentage cover at the neighborhood scale and correlating it with surface
temperature. What our data show is that two neighborhoods with identical canopy
percentages can differ by as much as 2.4°C in afternoon surface temperature,
depending on whether the canopy is distributed along streets or concentrated in
parks.

This has implications for municipal planting programs. Most programs are
evaluated on trees planted per year, a metric that treats every tree as
equivalent. Our results suggest that a street tree provides substantially more
cooling benefit per unit of canopy than a park tree, reflecting the fact that
street trees shade impervious surfaces during the hours when those surfaces
contribute most to the heat island effect.

Several limitations warrant discussion. Our measurements were collected during
a single summer, and interannual variation in precipitation is known to affect
canopy transpiration rates. The eleven neighborhoods in our sample are all
within a single metropolitan area, meaning our results may not generalize to
cities with different building geometries or background climates. It is also
the case that our surface temperature measurements were derived from satellite
imagery at 30-meter resolution, which is coarse relative to the street-scale
variation we are attempting to characterize.

Future work should address the temporal question directly. A multi-year
observation program would allow us to separate the effect of canopy placement
from the effect of precipitation variability, giving planners a firmer basis
for allocating a fixed planting budget. It would also be valuable to extend the
analysis to cities in arid climates, where irrigation cost may change the
calculation substantially.
