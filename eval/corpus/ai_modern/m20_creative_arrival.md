The train was forty minutes late, and by the time Oskar stepped onto the
platform the light had gone flat and grey, the way it does in that hour when
afternoon has ended but evening has not started. He set down the case and
looked for the sign he had been told to look for.

What he found instead was a woman in a green coat holding a piece of cardboard
with a name on it that was not his. She caught him reading it and shrugged,
apologetic, as though the mismatch were something she had caused.

He had rehearsed this arrival for weeks. In the version he had rehearsed there
was a car, and a short drive, and a conversation in which he explained himself
well. What there was instead was a platform, a woman in a green coat, and the
slow realization that nobody was coming.

The station café was open. He bought a coffee he did not want and sat where he
could see the entrance, and he watched the light go from grey to a deeper grey,
and he understood that he would have to decide something before it was fully
dark. The decision was not difficult. It was only that making it would mean
admitting the trip had been a mistake from the beginning, and he was not ready
to do that at four in the afternoon in a town he had never seen.

At half past five he picked up the case and walked out into the street. The
woman in the green coat was gone. The sign she had been holding was propped
against a bench, face up, the name on it still waiting for whoever it belonged
to.
